# Research shelf: the visual ear

> The reading list behind the ear. Nothing is redistributed here: every entry links to where the work lives, and says whether that copy is free.

Our composer is an LLM that writes chiptune scores as text, and it can't hear what it writes. The "visual ear" is a set of tools that read a score and put numbers on each bar: how tense it is, how dissonant, how surprising, how busy the rhythm is, and how much it repeats what came before. With those numbers we can tell the composer "too much here, too boring there". This shelf holds the research those numbers should come from. Each entry says what the work found or does and why it matters to us. Each ends with a link and then one of these:
- **free**, when the link leads to a free copy;
- **book** or **paywalled**, when it doesn't.

Works marked *(added)* weren't on the original list.

Where to start: Farbood 2012 (many features combined into one tension curve), Herremans & Chew 2016 with the Midi Miner code (tonal tension you can compute today), Pearce 2005 / IDyOM (note-by-note surprise), and Dai et al. 2022 (how much pop actually repeats).

## Tonal space and "note colors"

- **Lerdahl, F. (1988). Tonal pitch space. *Music Perception*, 5(3), 315–349.** The first version of Lerdahl's layered table (all 12 notes, then the scale, the triad, the fifth, and the root), which gives a number for how far any note, chord or key is from another. The 2007 tension model is built on these distances, so this is where "how far from home is this note" comes from. [Link](https://doi.org/10.2307/40285402) · **paywalled**
- **Lerdahl, F. (2001). *Tonal Pitch Space*. Oxford University Press.** The full theory: pitch-space distances, rules for how hard an unstable note pulls toward a stable neighbour, and a step-by-step way to compute tension through a piece. It is the most complete rule-based recipe for a tension number; the 2007 article below is the tested short version. [Link](https://academic.oup.com/book/32497) · **book**
- **Krumhansl, C. L., & Kessler, E. J. (1982). Tracing the dynamic changes in perceived tonal organization in a spatial representation of musical keys. *Psychological Review*, 89(4), 334–368.** Listeners rated how well each of the 12 notes fits after a passage that sets up a key, which produced the well-known major and minor "key profiles" (12 numbers each) and a map of key distances. These two tables are the cheapest useful "note colour" lookup we could build: how settled each scale degree feels, and the basis of standard key-finding. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.89.4.334) · **paywalled**
- **Krumhansl, C. L. (1990). *Cognitive Foundations of Musical Pitch*. Oxford University Press.** Collects the probe-tone studies: the key profiles, the stability ranking of notes, chords and keys, and the Krumhansl–Schmuckler key-finding method. It is the reference for what the profile numbers mean and how they were measured. [Link](https://academic.oup.com/book/40395) · **book**
- **Shepard, R. N. (1982). Geometrical approximations to the structure of musical pitch. *Psychological Review*, 89(4), 305–333.** Proposes helix shapes in which pitch height, octave sameness and the circle of fifths all become distances in one space. It explains why "map" models like the spiral array work, but it isn't something we would compute directly. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.89.4.305) · **paywalled**
- **Chew, E. (2000). *Towards a Mathematical Model of Tonality*. PhD thesis, Massachusetts Institute of Technology.** Introduces the spiral array, which places notes, chords and keys on nested helices so that any group of notes has a "centre", and its distance to each key gives a key estimate. Herremans and Chew's tension measures are built on this geometry, so this is where to get the coordinates and weights right. [Link](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/9139) · **free**
- **Chew, E. (2001). Modeling tonality: Applications to music cognition. *Proceedings of the 23rd Annual Conference of the Cognitive Science Society*.** *(added)* A short, readable summary of the spiral array and its key-finding method. Read it before the thesis. [Link](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/58w8w472) · **free**
- **Chew, E. (2014). *Mathematical and Computational Modeling of Tonality: Theory and Applications*. Springer (International Series in Operations Research & Management Science, vol. 204).** The book version of the spiral array, with the algorithms for key finding, splitting a piece into sections, and pitch spelling. It is the reference for implementation details. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4614-9475-1) · **book**
- **Cohn, R. (1998). Introduction to neo-Riemannian theory: A survey and a historical perspective. *Journal of Music Theory*, 42(2), 167–180.** Explains the Tonnetz, a grid on which neighbouring triads share two notes, and the three one-note moves between triads (P, L, R). Counting those moves gives a simple chord-to-chord distance that could flag "the harmony jumped a long way here". [Link](https://doi.org/10.2307/843871) · **paywalled**
- **Bernardes, G., Cocharro, D., Caetano, M., Guedes, C., & Davies, M. E. P. (2016). A multi-level tonal interval space for modelling pitch relatedness and musical consonance. *Journal of New Music Research*, 45(4), 281–294.** *(added)* Turns any set of notes into one point in a 12-dimensional space (a weighted Fourier transform of which pitch classes are present). Distances between points measure how related two chords or keys are, and distance from the centre measures consonance. It handles notes, chords and keys in one small piece of code, and two newer tension papers below are built on it. [Link](https://repositorio.inesctec.pt/handle/123456789/3897) · **free**

## Tension curves and generation

- **Lerdahl, F., & Krumhansl, C. L. (2007). Modeling tonal tension. *Music Perception*, 24(4), 329–366.** Tested Lerdahl's tension model against listeners' moment-by-moment ratings, and it predicted them well. The model has four parts: the piece's phrase structure, pitch-space distance, surface dissonance, and melodic attraction. It is the best-tested rule-based tension model, and those four parts are the menu for our own score. [Link](https://fredlerdahl.com/writings) · **free**
- **Farbood, M. M. (2006). *A Quantitative, Parametric Model of Musical Tension*. PhD thesis, MIT Media Lab.** Large listening studies on how harmony, melodic contour, loudness, tempo, note density and other features combine into felt tension, with a model built from the results. It is the long version of the 2012 paper, with every experiment in full. [Link](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/34182) · **free**
- **Farbood, M. M. (2012). A parametric, temporal model of musical tension. *Music Perception*, 29(4), 387–428.** Combines harmony, pitch height, melodic expectation, loudness, note density, tempo and rhythm into one tension curve. A plain weighted sum didn't match listeners, but a model that tracks each feature's recent rise or fall over a moving time window matched them closely. This is the closest published thing to the visual ear: one curve from many features, with a tested method for weighting and smoothing them. [Link](https://wp.nyu.edu/mfarbood/publications/) · **free**
- **Granot, R. Y., & Eitan, Z. (2011). Musical tension and the interaction of dynamic auditory parameters. *Music Perception*, 28(3), 219–246.** Listeners rated tension in short melodies that varied pitch direction, register, loudness and tempo. Loudness, pitch direction and register changed tension and tempo didn't, and the effects interacted rather than simply adding up. This warns against a naive sum of features, and suggests register and volume matter as much as harmony in a chip tune. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2011.28.3.219) · **paywalled**
- **Bigand, E., Parncutt, R., & Lerdahl, F. (1996). Perception of musical tension in short chord sequences: The influence of harmonic function, sensory dissonance, horizontal motion, and musical training. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 58(1), 125–141.** *(added)* An early test of which ingredients drive the tension listeners hear in chord progressions, finding harmonic function weighed more than raw dissonance. It is background for how to weight the parts of a chord-tension score. [Link](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03205482) · **paywalled**
- **Herremans, D., & Chew, E. (2016). Tension ribbons: Quantifying and visualising tonal tension. *Proceedings of TENOR 2016 (2nd International Conference on Technologies for Music Notation and Representation)*, Cambridge, UK, 8–18.** Computes three numbers per time window on the spiral array: how spread out the notes are (roughly dissonance), how far the harmony moved since the last window, and how far the notes are from the key. They are simple to compute and are drawn as ribbons over the score, which is nearly the display we want. [Link](https://dorienherremans.com/content/tension-ribbons-quantifying-and-visualising-tonal-tension) · **free**
- **Herremans, D., & Chew, E. (2019). MorpheuS: Generating structured music with constrained patterns and tension. *IEEE Transactions on Affective Computing*, 10(4), 510–523 (arXiv:1812.04832).** A generator that keeps a template piece's repeated-pattern structure and searches for notes that follow a target tension curve, scored with the tension-ribbon measures. It is our loop in another form: a composer steered by a tension score plus a repetition plan. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.04832) · **free**
- **Herremans, D., & Chew, E. (2019). Towards emotion based music generation: A tonal tension model based on the spiral array. *Proceedings of the 41st Annual Conference of the Cognitive Science Society (CogSci 2019)*, Montreal.** A two-page summary of the three tension measures and how MorpheuS uses them to steer generation. Use it as the quick overview. [Link](https://www.dorienherremans.com/content/towards-emotion-based-music-generation-tonal-tension-model-based-spiral-array) · **free**
- **Guo, R., Simpson, I., Magnusson, T., Kiefer, C., & Herremans, D. (2020). A variational autoencoder for music generation controlled by tonal tension. *Joint Conference on AI Music Creativity (CSMC + MuMe 2020)*; arXiv:2010.06230.** *(added)* A neural generator whose tension (two spiral-array measures) can be turned up or down while it keeps the rhythm. Another example of tension used as a control knob. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06230) · **free**
- **Guo, R., Herremans, D., & Magnusson, T. (2019). Midi Miner: A Python library for tonal tension and track classification. *ISMIR 2019 Late-Breaking/Demo*; arXiv:1910.02049.** *(added)* Open-source Python that computes the three tension-ribbon measures from a MIDI file, estimates the key, and labels tracks as melody, bass or harmony. It is the quickest working code to start our tonal-tension measure from ([github.com/ruiguo-bio/midi-miner](https://github.com/ruiguo-bio/midi-miner)). [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02049) · **free**
- **Navarro-Cáceres, M., Caetano, M., Bernardes, G., Sánchez-Barba, M., & Merchán Sánchez-Jara, J. (2020). A computational model of tonal tension profile of chord progressions in the Tonal Interval Space. *Entropy*, 22(11), 1291.** *(added)* A chord-by-chord tension curve built from four parts with weights fitted to listeners: dissonance 0.402, hierarchical tension 0.246, distance from the key 0.202, voice leading 0.193. On 12 progressions it beat Lerdahl's model and MorpheuS, so it gives us a ready formula with published weights to start from. [Link](https://doi.org/10.3390/e22111291) · **free**
- **Ebrahimzadeh, M., Bernardes, G., & Stober, S. (2025). Explicit tonal tension conditioning via dual-level beam search for symbolic music generation. *Proceedings of CMMR 2025*; arXiv:2511.19342.** *(added)* A Transformer proposes candidate bars, and each is re-ranked by a computed tension score so the piece follows a target tension curve, and listeners heard the intended tension. It isn't an LLM, but it is our loop on a small scale: measure tension bar by bar and keep the version that fits the curve. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19342) · **free**

## Expectation, surprise, pleasure

- **Meyer, L. B. (1956). *Emotion and Meaning in Music*. University of Chicago Press.** The founding idea that feeling in music comes from expectations being set up, delayed and resolved. It is the reason a "surprise" score makes sense at all. [Link](https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226521374.001.0001) · **book**
- **Narmour, E. (1990). *The Analysis and Cognition of Basic Melodic Structures: The Implication-Realization Model*. University of Chicago Press.** Turns note-to-note expectation into rules, for example that a small interval implies another small step the same way and a big leap implies a turn back. The rules can be checked note by note to flag melodies that break them all the time, or never. [Link](https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780226568454) · **book**
- **Schellenberg, E. G. (1997). Simplifying the implication-realization model of melodic expectancy. *Music Perception*, 14(3), 295–318.** Showed that Narmour's five rules can be cut to two, stay close in pitch and turn back after a leap, and still predict listeners as well. That makes it the cheapest usable "is this next note expected?" score. [Link](https://doi.org/10.2307/40285723) · **paywalled**
- **Margulis, E. H. (2005). A model of melodic expectation. *Music Perception*, 22(4), 663–714.** *(added)* Turns note-by-note expectation into three kinds of felt tension: surprise, denial and anticipation. It is one of the few published formulas that goes from "was this expected?" to a tension curve. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2005.22.4.663) · **paywalled**
- **Huron, D. (2006). *Sweet Anticipation: Music and the Psychology of Expectation*. MIT Press.** A plain account of how prediction creates musical feeling, with chapters on syncopation, cadences and the balance between familiar and surprising. It is the best single book for the vocabulary we'll give the composer. [Link](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/6575.001.0001) · **book**
- **Berlyne, D. E. (1971). *Aesthetics and Psychobiology*. Appleton-Century-Crofts.** The source of the "inverted U": people like medium complexity best, with too simple boring and too complex unpleasant. That is exactly the "too much here, too boring there" target. [Link](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL7597817W) · **book**
- **Pearce, M. T. (2005). *The construction and evaluation of statistical models of melodic structure in music perception and composition*. PhD thesis, City University London.** Builds the model later called IDyOM, which learns from a collection of melodies and gives every note of a new one a probability. That yields "surprise" (information content) and "uncertainty" (entropy) note by note, the main recipe for a computable surprise curve. [Link](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/8459/) · **free**
- **Pearce, M. T., & Wiggins, G. A. (2006). Expectation in melody: The influence of context and learning. *Music Perception*, 23(5), 377–405.** *(added)* The main test showing that IDyOM's learned probabilities predict listeners' melodic expectations better than Narmour-style rules. It is the evidence for choosing a learned model over hand-written rules. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2006.23.5.377) · **free**
- **Pearce, M. T. (2018). Statistical learning and probabilistic prediction in music cognition: Mechanisms of stylistic enculturation. *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences*, 1423(1), 378–395.** A readable open-access review of how one prediction model (IDyOM) accounts for expectation, emotion, memory, phrase boundaries and meter. It is the best short introduction before building on IDyOM. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.13654) · **free**
- **Temperley, D. (2008). A probabilistic model of melody perception. *Cognitive Science*, 32(2), 418–444.** A small model with three ingredients (melodies stay in a range, move by small steps, and fit the key) that scores how probable a melody is. It is a simple, transparent alternative to IDyOM for a "how normal is this tune" number. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1080/03640210701864089) · the 2008 journal version is **free**; the saved file is Temperley's same-titled ISMIR 2006 conference version: **free**
- **Temperley, D. (2007). *Music and Probability*. MIT Press.** The full version of the probabilistic approach, covering key, meter and melody, plus a chapter on how composers use probability. [Link](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/4807.001.0001) · **book**
- **Cheung, V. K. M., Harrison, P. M. C., Meyer, L., Pearce, M. T., Haynes, J.-D., & Koelsch, S. (2019). Uncertainty and surprise jointly predict musical pleasure and amygdala, hippocampus, and auditory cortex activity. *Current Biology*, 29(23), 4084–4092.** Ran IDyOM over about 80,000 chords from 745 Billboard songs. A chord pleased most when listeners were confident and it surprised them, or when they were unsure and it was safe. So surprise should be judged against how predictable the context was, not on its own. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067) · **free**
- **Gold, B. P., Pearce, M. T., Mas-Herrero, E., Dagher, A., & Zatorre, R. J. (2019). Predictability and uncertainty in the pleasure of music: A reward for learning? *Journal of Neuroscience*, 39(47), 9397–9409.** Using IDyOM's numbers on real music, liking peaked at medium predictability, and in uncertain passages people preferred the expected note. This measures Berlyne's inverted U with the same numbers we would compute. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0428-19.2019) (also [PMC6867811](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811/)) · **free**
- **Jakubowski, K., Finkel, S., Stewart, L., & Müllensiefen, D. (2017). Dissecting an earworm: Melodic features and song popularity predict involuntary musical imagery. *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*, 11(2), 122–135.** Songs that stick in the head were faster, more successful in the charts, and had a common overall melodic shape with unusual interval patterns inside it. "Familiar shape, unusual detail" is a usable hook heuristic. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1037/aca0000090) · **paywalled** (the accepted manuscript is free as a Word file at [Goldsmiths](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/19405/))

## Dissonance

Which table to copy: for a ready interval or chord lookup, use Bowling et al. 2018 (listener ratings of every chord within an octave) or Huron 1994 (one number per interval class). For computing it from the sound, use Sethares 1993 with Plomp & Levelt's curve, or `incon` from Harrison & Pearce 2020.

- **Plomp, R., & Levelt, W. J. M. (1965). Tonal consonance and critical bandwidth. *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America*, 38(4), 548–560.** The classic roughness curve: two pure tones sound roughest at about a quarter of a critical band apart, and smooth once they are a full band apart. Every roughness calculator starts from it, including one we would run on square-wave overtones. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1909741) · **free**
- **Sethares, W. A. (1993). Local consonance and the relationship between timbre and scale. *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America*, 94(3), 1218–1228.** Gives a simple formula version of the Plomp–Levelt curve and shows that dissonance depends on a sound's overtones, not only the interval. That matters for chiptune: square and pulse waves are heavy in odd overtones, so some intervals will sound rougher than piano-based tables say. [Link](https://sethares.engr.wisc.edu/papers/consance.html) · **free**
- **Harrison, P. M. C., & Pearce, M. T. (2020). Simultaneous consonance in music perception and composition. *Psychological Review*, 127(2), 216–244.** *(added)* Compared many consonance models against listener data and real music, and found consonance is mainly roughness, harmonicity and familiarity. Their `incon` R package implements the models, so it is the most practical way to score any chord. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000169) · **free**
- **Bowling, D. L., Purves, D., & Gill, K. Z. (2018). Vocal similarity predicts the relative attraction of musical chords. *PNAS*, 115(1), 216–221.** *(added)* Listeners rated every two-, three- and four-note chord within an octave, so the supplementary data is a ready lookup table of how pleasant each chord sounds. This is the one we could copy straight in. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1713206115) · **free**
- **McDermott, J. H., Lehr, A. J., & Oxenham, A. J. (2010). Individual differences reveal the basis of consonance. *Current Biology*, 20(11), 1035–1041.** More than 250 listeners rated intervals and chords; how much they preferred consonance matched how much they liked harmonic overtones, not how much they disliked beating. The ratings make a good interval ranking, though you'd have to read them off a graph. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2010.04.019) · **free**
- **Huron, D. (1994). Interval-class content in equally tempered pitch-class sets: Common scales exhibit optimum tonal consonance. *Music Perception*, 11(3), 289–305.** *(added)* Gives one consonance number for each of the six interval classes, averaged from earlier studies, and uses them to score scales. It is the smallest possible lookup table for interval dissonance. [Link](https://doi.org/10.2307/40285624) · **paywalled**
- **Hutchinson, W., & Knopoff, L. (1978). The acoustic component of Western consonance. *Interface*, 7(1), 1–29.** *(added)* A worked method for computing the roughness of chords from their overtones, with published values for intervals and triads; it is one of the models in `incon`. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1080/09298217808570246) · **paywalled**

## Rhythm

- **Longuet-Higgins, H. C., & Lee, C. S. (1984). The rhythmic interpretation of monophonic music. *Music Perception*, 1(4), 424–441.** Gives the classic syncopation score: each position in the bar gets a strength weight, and a note held over (or a rest on) a stronger position counts as a syncopation, sized by the difference. It is the simplest per-bar syncopation number, and most later measures are variants of it. [Link](https://doi.org/10.2307/40285271) · **paywalled**; an earlier version is free from [KTH](https://www.speech.kth.se/music/publications/kma/kma39.html) (in *Studies of Music Performance*, ed. J. Sundberg, 1983, pp. 7–26): **free** (the whole volume; the paper starts on PDF page 8)
- **Smith, L. M., & Honing, H. (2006). Evaluating and extending computational models of rhythmic syncopation in music. *Proceedings of the International Computer Music Conference (ICMC 2006)*, 688–691.** *(added)* Compared the Longuet-Higgins & Lee score with listeners' ratings of rhythmic complexity; they agree, but with clear outliers. A warning not to treat one syncopation number as the whole of "rhythmic complexity". [Link](https://www.mcg.uva.nl/mcg-2023/abstracts/smith-honing-2006.html) · **free**
- **Witek, M. A. G., Clarke, E. F., Wallentin, M., Kringelbach, M. L., & Vuust, P. (2014). Syncopation, body-movement and pleasure in groove music. *PLoS ONE*, 9(4), e94446.** Listeners rated funk drum breaks with different amounts of syncopation, and a medium amount gave the most pleasure and the strongest urge to move. So a syncopation meter needs a target band, not "more is better". (A correction was published in 2015, *PLoS ONE* 10(9), e0139409.) [Link](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094446) · **free**
- **Fitch, W. T., & Rosenfeld, A. J. (2007). Perception and production of syncopated rhythms. *Music Perception*, 25(1), 43–58.** When people tapped along to increasingly syncopated rhythms (graded with the Longuet-Higgins & Lee score), they began to shift their beat and remembered the rhythms less well. Past some level, the listener loses the beat, which is a natural "too much" ceiling. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2007.25.1.43) · **paywalled**
- **Gómez, F., Melvin, A., Rappaport, D., & Toussaint, G. T. (2005). Mathematical measures of syncopation. *Renaissance Banff: Bridges 2005 Proceedings*, 73–84.** Proposes the "weighted note-to-beat distance", a syncopation score based on how far each note falls from the nearest beat, and compares it with older measures. A second syncopation measure that is easy to code. [Link](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2005/bridges2005-73.html) · **free**
- **Song, C., Simpson, A. J. R., Harte, C. A., Pearce, M. T., & Sandler, M. B. (2013). Syncopation and the score. *PLoS ONE*, 8(9), e74692.** Musicians rated how syncopated short notated rhythms sounded: 6/8 felt more syncopated than 4/4, and missing downbeats added the most. These rhythms make good test cases for checking that our syncopation meter agrees with human ears. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0074692) · **free**
- **Song, C., Pearce, M., & Harte, C. (2015). SynPy: A Python toolkit for syncopation modelling. *Proceedings of the 12th Sound and Music Computing Conference (SMC 2015)*, Maynooth.** *(added)* Open-source Python implementing seven syncopation measures (Longuet-Higgins & Lee, Pressing, Toussaint, Sioros & Guedes, Keith, Gómez's weighted note-to-beat distance, and more), reporting bar by bar. It is probably the fastest route to a working syncopation meter. [Link](https://zenodo.org/records/851079) · **free**
- **Toussaint, G. T. (2004). A comparison of rhythmic similarity measures. *Proceedings of the 5th International Conference on Music Information Retrieval (ISMIR 2004)*, Barcelona, 242–245.** Compares simple rhythm distances (Hamming, swap, chronotonic, interval-vector) on families of clave and bell patterns. They are cheap ways to say "bar 5 has bar 1's rhythm" or "this is a small rhythmic variation". [Link](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2004/paper/000134.pdf) · **free**
- **Gómez-Marín, D., Jordà, S., & Herrera, P. (2015). PAD and SAD: Two awareness-weighted rhythmic similarity distances. *Proceedings of the 16th ISMIR Conference*, Málaga, 666–672.** Two rhythm-similarity distances that weight each position in the bar by how much listeners notice it, matching human similarity ratings better than older distances. Useful for deciding whether two bars "sound like the same rhythm". [Link](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2015/paper/000185.pdf) · **free**
- **Toussaint, G. T. (2013). *The Geometry of Musical Rhythm: What Makes a "Good" Rhythm Good?* CRC Press (2nd ed. 2019).** A readable survey of ways to measure rhythm (evenness, off-beatness, syncopation, complexity, similarity), mostly on world-music timelines. The 2nd edition adds chapters on meter and metric complexity, which are the parts we need. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781351247771) · **book**

## Phrase form and repetition

- **Caplin, W. E. (1998). *Classical Form: A Theory of Formal Functions for the Instrumental Music of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven*. Oxford University Press.** The standard modern account of phrase types (sentence, period) and of what each part of a phrase does. It gives us names and templates such as "state the idea, repeat it, then break it up and drive to the cadence", a shape we could test a melody for. [Link](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/classical-form-9780195143997) · **book**
- **Schoenberg, A. (1967). *Fundamentals of Musical Composition* (G. Strang & L. Stein, Eds.). Faber and Faber.** A composition textbook, published after Schoenberg's death, built on the motive, varied repetition, the sentence and the period. It gives us practical words for telling the composer how to vary a motive instead of repeating it exactly. [Link](https://www.faber.co.uk/product/9780571196586-fundamentals-of-musical-composition/) · **book**
- **Margulis, E. H. (2014). *On Repeat: How Music Plays the Mind*. Oxford University Press.** Argues that repetition is central to how music is heard: repeating a passage changes how it sounds and makes it seem more musical. It supports keeping repetition in a healthy band rather than minimising it. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199990825.001.0001) · **book**
- **Müllensiefen, D., & Frieler, K. (2004). Cognitive adequacy in the measurement of melodic similarity: Algorithmic vs. human judgments. *Computing in Musicology*, 13, 147–176.** Tested many melodic-similarity algorithms against expert ratings of short pop-melody variants and found the combination that best matches people. It is the paper to follow when we score how alike two phrases are in pitch. [Link](https://www.ccarh.org/publications/cm/13/abstract13-8.html) · **free** (the publisher's server was down)
- **Müllensiefen, D., & Frieler, K. (2004). Optimizing measures of melodic similarity for the exploration of a large folk song database. *Proceedings of the 5th ISMIR*, Barcelona, 274–280.** The companion paper: about 50 similarity measures compared with expert ratings, the best combined and checked on a folk-song collection. [Link](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2004/paper/000178.pdf) · **free**
- **Dai, S., Yu, H., & Dannenberg, R. B. (2022). What is missing in deep music generation? A study of repetition and structure in popular music. *Proceedings of the 23rd ISMIR*, Bengaluru; arXiv:2209.00182.** *(added)* Measured repetition in Chinese (POP909) and American pop. In the American set, 28% of phrases follow a bar-level rhythm scheme like abab or aba, and 7% use one rhythm in every bar. In POP909, more than half of phrases repeat immediately, and in 79% of songs only 15–35% of the duration is new material. Pitch surprise is highest at section starts (over 3.5 bits) and lowest mid-phrase (2.6), which makes it the best source of numbers for a "too repetitive / not repetitive enough" meter. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00182) · **free**
- **Zou, Y., Zou, P., Zhao, Y., Zhang, K., Zhang, R., & Wang, X. (2022). MELONS: Generating melody with long-term structure using transformers and structure graph. *Proceedings of IEEE ICASSP 2022*, 191–195.** *(added)* Labels how each bar of a pop melody relates to an earlier one (exact repeat, transposition, same rhythm with similar or new pitches, and so on) and counts them. In POP909, 44% of bars exactly repeat an earlier bar, about 10% reuse an earlier rhythm with changed pitches, and 81% relate to some earlier bar. Wikifonia gives similar figures (47%, about 15%, 81%). This is the most direct count of rhythm-versus-pitch repetition in pop we found, and the bar relations are simple to compute. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05020) · **free** (the 2021 arXiv version)
- **Dai, S., Zhang, H., & Dannenberg, R. B. (2020). Automatic analysis and influence of hierarchical structure on melody, rhythm and harmony in popular music. *Proceedings of the Joint Conference on AI Music Creativity (CSMC-MuMe 2020)*; arXiv:2010.07518.** *(added)* Finds phrases and sections in POP909 songs from repetition alone, then shows what happens at phrase ends: long notes, V–I and the tonic gather at the ends of sections. These are concrete targets for "phrase ends should breathe and land". Code and annotations are at [github.com/Dsqvival/hierarchical-structure-analysis](https://github.com/Dsqvival/hierarchical-structure-analysis). [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.07518) · **free**
- **Dai, S., Zhang, H., & Dannenberg, R. B. (2024). The interconnections of music structure, harmony, melody, rhythm, and predictivity. *Music & Science*, 7.** *(added)* The journal version of the 2020 paper, extended with how pop structure changed over the decades. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1177/20592043241234758) · **free**

## Melodic complexity scores *(added)*

- **Eerola, T. (2016). Expectancy-violation and information-theoretic models of melodic complexity. *Empirical Musicology Review*, 11(1), 2–17.** Compared rule-based complexity formulas with IDyOM-style surprise models on seven sets of listener ratings (folk, pop and artificial). The models explained 46–74% of the ratings, and a simplified rule-based model (tonal ambiguity, pitch proximity and rhythmic variability) was the most efficient, so this is the most directly usable validated complexity formula. The rated melodies are on the [OSU Knowledge Bank](https://hdl.handle.net/1811/77849). [Link](https://doi.org/10.18061/emr.v11i1.4836) · **free**
- **Eerola, T., Himberg, T., Toiviainen, P., & Louhivuori, J. (2006). Perceived complexity of western and African folk melodies by western and African listeners. *Psychology of Music*, 34(3), 337–371.** The original cross-cultural test of the rule-based complexity model: it worked well when listeners knew the style and less well when they didn't. A reminder that "complex" is relative to what the listener is used to. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735606064842) · **paywalled**
- **Eerola, T., & Toiviainen, P. (2004; v1.1 2016). *MIDI Toolbox: MATLAB Tools for Music Research* (manual). University of Jyväskylä.** Free MATLAB code with ready-made melody measures: expectancy-based complexity, Narmour scores, Krumhansl–Kessler key finding, melodic attraction and entropy. Even if we never touch MATLAB, the manual documents formulas we can port. [Link](https://github.com/miditoolbox/1.1) · **free**
- **Sauvé, S. A., & Pearce, M. T. (2019). Information-theoretic modeling of perceived musical complexity. *Music Perception*, 37(2), 165–178.** Listeners' complexity ratings tracked IDyOM's average surprise, mostly melodic and harmonic, and musical training made no difference. It supports using average surprise as the "how busy is this" dial. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2019.37.2.165) · **free**
- **Hamilton, M., & Pearce, M. (2024). Trajectories and revolutions in popular melody based on U.S. charts from 1950 to 2023. *Scientific Reports*, 14, 14749.** Computed eight simple melody features (tonal strength, pitch and rhythm unpredictability, pitch spread, interval size, note density and others) on 1,131 transcribed Billboard top-5 melodies. Melodies got simpler and denser over time, with jumps around 1975 and 2000. That gives a ready feature list plus real pop numbers to compare our tunes against. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-64571-x) · **free**

## Datasets of pop melodies, chords and sections

- **Burgoyne, J. A., Wild, J., & Fujinaga, I. (2011). An expert ground-truth set for audio chord recognition and music analysis. *Proceedings of the 12th ISMIR*, Miami, 633–638.** Experts transcribed chords, meter, phrases and sections for songs sampled at random from the Billboard Hot 100 (1958–1991), so it shows ordinary pop and not just the biggest hits. It is a baseline for normal chord surprise and section lengths. Cheung et al. 2019 used its songs. [Paper](https://ismir2011.ismir.net/papers/OS8-1.pdf) · [Dataset](https://ddmal.ca/research/The_McGill_Billboard_Project_(Chord_Analysis_Dataset)/) · **free**
  License/terms: CC0 (public domain), with a request to cite the paper. It has chords and structure but no melody, covering 890 chart slots (740 distinct songs).
- **Hooktheory TheoryTab (website).** User-entered melody-plus-chord transcriptions of song sections, written in scale degrees and Roman numerals. This is the closest public format to what our composer writes. [Link](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab) · website
  License/terms: Hooktheory's [terms](https://www.hooktheory.com/terms) forbid scraping, bulk download, and using the data to train or evaluate models without a written license, so browse it by hand only.
- **Donahue, C., Thickstun, J., & Liang, P. (2022). Melody transcription via generative pre-training. *Proceedings of the 23rd ISMIR*, Bengaluru; arXiv:2212.01884.** Built a dataset of about 50 hours of beat-aligned melody and chord annotations from HookTheory to train a melody transcriber (Sheet Sage). This is the legitimate route to Hooktheory-style data for statistics like typical interval sizes or how often melody notes are chord tones. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.01884) · [Code/data](https://github.com/chrisdonahue/sheetsage) · **free**
  License/terms: the dataset and models are CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 (non-commercial, share-alike); the code is MIT.
- **Wang, Z., Chen, K., Jiang, J., Zhang, Y., Xu, M., Dai, S., Gu, X., & Xia, G. (2020). POP909: A pop-song dataset for music arrangement generation. *Proceedings of the 21st ISMIR*, Montréal; arXiv:2008.07142.** 909 Chinese pop songs as piano-arrangement MIDI, with the sung melody on its own track plus beat, chord and key labels. These are real pop melodies to calibrate our scores against; phrase and section labels come from Dai et al. 2020 (above). [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07142) · [Dataset](https://github.com/music-x-lab/POP909-Dataset) · **free**
  License/terms: the repository is MIT-licensed, but the songs themselves are commercial compositions.
- **de Clercq, T., & Temperley, D. (2011). A corpus analysis of rock harmony. *Popular Music*, 30(1), 47–70.** Both authors analysed 100 songs from Rolling Stone's "500 Greatest Songs" list in Roman numerals. IV is the most common chord after I and most often goes straight to I. The corpus has since grown to 200 songs (RS200) with melody transcriptions, giving scale-degree tables for rock melody and harmony, which is the "expected" baseline a surprise score needs. [Paper (authors' copy)](https://rockcorpus.midside.com/2011_paper/declercq_temperley_2011.pdf) · [Corpus](https://rockcorpus.midside.com/) · **free**
  License/terms: the corpus files are CC BY 4.0 (free use with credit); the article is © Cambridge University Press, hosted by the authors.
- **Schaffrath, H. (1995). *The Essen Folksong Collection in the Humdrum Kern Format* (D. Huron, Ed.). Center for Computer Assisted Research in the Humanities.** Thousands of single-line folk tunes, mostly German, with phrase endings marked. It is the standard test set for melody models (IDyOM, Temperley and FANTASTIC all used it), and the phrase marks show how long a natural phrase runs. [GitHub (CCARH)](https://github.com/ccarh/essen-folksong-collection) · [KernScores](https://kern.humdrum.org/cgi-bin/browse?l=essen) · dataset, no paper
  License/terms: CCARH allows academic research use only, with no redistribution or commercial use; music21 ships a copy for non-commercial use.
- **Raffel, C. (2016). *Learning-based methods for comparing sequences, with applications to audio-to-MIDI alignment and matching*. PhD thesis, Columbia University.** The thesis behind the Lakh MIDI Dataset: 176,581 unique MIDI files from the web, 45,129 matched to real recordings. It is huge and noisy (amateur files, every genre), good for bulk statistics like common rhythm patterns or how often bars repeat, not as a clean reference. [Thesis](https://colinraffel.com/publications/thesis.pdf) · [Dataset](https://colinraffel.com/projects/lmd/) · **free**
  License/terms: the dataset is CC BY 4.0, but the songs are copyrighted compositions transcribed by unknown people.

## Tools

- **music21** (Cuthbert, M. S., & Ariza, C. (2010). music21: A toolkit for computer-aided musicology and symbolic music data. *Proceedings of the 11th ISMIR*, Utrecht, 637–642). A Python library that reads ABC, MusicXML, MIDI and Humdrum and handles the theory chores: keys, intervals, Roman numerals, beat strength. It is the natural parser and plumbing for the visual ear. [Site](https://web.mit.edu/music21/) · [GitHub](https://github.com/cuthbertLab/music21) · **free**
- **IDyOM** (Pearce). A Common Lisp program (GPL-3) that learns note probabilities from a corpus and reports, for every note of a new melody, how surprising it was and how uncertain the listener was just before it. It is the standard surprise meter; the theory is Pearce 2005 above. [GitHub](https://github.com/mtpearce/idyom) · software
- **GraphIDyOM** (Bono Rosselló, L. (2026). GraphIDyOM: A graph-native Python reimplementation of IDyOM for musical expectation modelling. arXiv:2607.25787). *(added)* A Python rewrite of IDyOM, checked against the Lisp original. It is probably the easiest way to get IDyOM surprise into our pipeline without installing Lisp. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25787) · **free**
- **FANTASTIC** (Müllensiefen, D. (2009). *Fantastic: Feature ANalysis Technology Accessing STatistics (In a Corpus): Technical Report v1.5*. Goldsmiths, University of London). An R program that computes summary features of a melody (range, interval sizes, contour, implied key) and "m-types", short melodic figures scored for how repetitive or unusual they are against a corpus. The m-type features are a ready-made repetition and novelty score. [PDF](https://www.doc.gold.ac.uk/isms/m4s/FANTASTIC_docs.pdf) · **free**
- **jSymbolic** (McKay, C., Cumming, J. E., & Fujinaga, I. (2018). jSymbolic 2.2: Extracting features from symbolic music for use in musicological and MIR research. *Proceedings of the 19th ISMIR*, Paris). A Java program that computes 246 features (pitch statistics, intervals, chord types, rhythm, texture) per piece or per window. It is good for broad "what kind of piece is this" profiles and less suited to following a melody note by note. [Site](https://jmir.sourceforge.net/jSymbolic.html) · **free**
- **melody-features** (Whyatt, D. M., & Harrison, P. M. C. (2026). Computational features for symbolic melody analysis. arXiv:2608.19061). *(added)* Reviews the existing melody-feature toolboxes, sorts their features into one scheme, and reimplements them all in one Python package. It is likely the quickest way to get dozens of established melody measures without porting R, Java or MATLAB. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19061) · [GitHub](https://github.com/dmwhyatt/melody-features) · **free**
- See also, elsewhere on this shelf: Midi Miner (tonal tension, Python), SynPy (syncopation, Python), `incon` (consonance, R; Harrison & Pearce 2020), MIDI Toolbox (complexity, MATLAB).

## LLMs composing in text notation

- **Yuan, R., Lin, H., Wang, Y., et al. (2024). ChatMusician: Understanding and generating music intrinsically with LLM. *Findings of ACL 2024*, 6252–6271; arXiv:2402.16153.** Trained LLaMA-2 further on music in ABC text and showed an LLM can write well-formed, structured tunes as plain text. On their music-theory test, though, even GPT-4 did only a little better than chance, so ABC works as a medium but the model's own musical judgement is weak, which is the case for an outside ear. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16153) · **free**
- **Deng, Q., Yang, Q., Yuan, R., Huang, Y., et al. (2024). ComposerX: Multi-agent symbolic music composition with LLMs. *Proceedings of the 25th ISMIR*, San Francisco; arXiv:2404.18081.** Several GPT-4 agents (leader, melody, harmony, instruments, reviewer, arrangement) pass an ABC score around over revision rounds. It is the closest published design to ours, except that the reviewer is another LLM reading ABC rather than a measuring tool. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18081) · **free**
- **Xing, P., Plaat, A., & van Stein, N. (2025). CoComposer: LLM multi-agent collaborative music composition. arXiv:2509.00132.** *(added)* Five LLM agents follow a composer's workflow and review each other's ABC, and it beat earlier multi-agent systems on an automatic aesthetics score. The critique is still one LLM's opinion of another's work, which shows what our measured feedback would replace. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.00132) · **free**
- **Wang, B., Vishe, Y., Xu, X., Novack, Z., Jiang, X., McAuley, J., & Wu, J. (2025). CSyMR: Benchmarking compositional music information retrieval in symbolic music reasoning. arXiv:2601.11556.** *(added)* An LLM that called music21 analysis tools answered questions about scores 5–7 points more accurately than the same LLM reading the score alone, with the biggest gains on analysis-heavy questions. Direct evidence that an LLM does better when a tool does the counting, which is the premise of the visual ear. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11556) · **free**
- **Xu, Y. (2026). Libretto: Giving LLM agents a sense of musical structure. arXiv:2606.22708.** *(added)* An LLM-friendly score format plus a scorer that compares each piece with corpus statistics for rhythm, harmony, melody, texture, form and variation, and feeds the results back so the agent can diagnose and revise its own piece. It is close to the visual ear already built, so its measurement axes and the way it words feedback are worth reading closely. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22708) · **free**
- **Liu, H., & Tang, X. (2026). Do language models need music supervision? Verifiable rewards for multi-constraint symbolic music generation. arXiv:2609.23665.** *(added)* Plain code checks on ABC output (key, metre, length, range, final note, rhythm, melodic motion, form) were used as the reward to train a small LLM. That took it from meeting about 16% of mixed constraint sets to about 80%, with partial credit working better than pass/fail, which bears on how our scores should be graded. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23665) · **free**
- **Karystinaios, E., & Widmer, G. (2026). ScorePrompts: Natural-language exploration of symbolic music scores through analysis. *ISMIR 2026 Late-Breaking Demo*; arXiv:2609.13291.** *(added)* Analysis models run first (harmony, key, cadences, phrase boundaries, texture), and a constrained LLM then turns the results into plain descriptions with the numbers attached. That "analyse first, then talk" pattern is how the ear should hand its findings to the composer: bar by bar, with evidence. [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13291) · **free**
